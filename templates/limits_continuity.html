Limits & Continuity

$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ means you can make $f(x)$ as close to $L$ as anyone demands, just by taking $x$ close enough to $a$. What happens exactly at $x = a$ doesn't matter. A function is continuous at $a$ when the limit exists and equals $f(a)$: no jumps, no holes, no surprises.

1 The ε–δ game

Think of it as a game between two players. A challenger picks a tolerance $\varepsilon$ (the horizontal band around $L$). You must answer with a $\delta$ (the vertical band around $a$) so that every $x$ in that band, except possibly $a$ itself, has $f(x)$ inside the $\varepsilon$-band. If you can always answer, however small $\varepsilon$ gets, then $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$. $$\forall \varepsilon>0\;\; \exists \delta>0:\quad 0<|x-a|<\delta \;\Rightarrow\; |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon$$

Questions to ask the student
  1. For $x^2$: as ε halves, roughly how does the δ you need change? (It halves too, near $a=1$: $\delta \approx \varepsilon/2$.)
  2. For $\sin(x)/x$: $f(0)$ isn't even defined. Why doesn't that stop the limit from existing?
  3. For the jump: find an $L$ and ε where you can win. Why does that not prove the limit exists?
  4. For $\sin(1/x)$: zoom in as far as you like. Why can no $L$ ever work for ε = 0.5?
  5. $x\sin(1/x)$ oscillates infinitely often too. Why does it have a limit? (It is squeezed between $-|x|$ and $|x|$.)

3 Ways to be discontinuous

$f$ is continuous at $a$ when all three of these hold: (1) $f(a)$ is defined, (2) $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ exists, and (3) they are equal. Each picture below breaks a different condition.

4 The derivative is a limit

The slope of a curve at $a$ is the limit of slopes of secant lines: $$f'(a) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}.$$ Every gradient used to train a neural network is one of these limits. Shrink $h$ and watch the secants turn into the tangent. Then keep going, and watch the computer start to get it wrong.

5 Why machine learning cares

The original perceptron used the step function. Its gradient is 0 everywhere (and undefined at 0), so gradient descent gets no signal about which way to move. Replacing it with the continuous, differentiable sigmoid is what made backpropagation possible.

Gradients need continuity

Gradient descent assumes that a small change in the weights gives a small change in the loss. That is exactly what continuity promises. Derivatives (Section 4) need even more: smoothness.

Why we don't optimize accuracy

Accuracy jumps in steps of $1/N$ as the weights change: it is piecewise constant, with gradient 0 almost everywhere. So we train on cross-entropy, a continuous stand-in, and only report accuracy.

ReLU's corner

$\max(0,x)$ is continuous but has no derivative at 0: the left limit of the slope is 0 and the right is 1. Frameworks simply pick 0 there (a "subgradient"). Try ReLU at $a = 0$ in Section 4.

Lipschitz continuity & robustness

If $|f(x)-f(y)| \le K|x-y|$, then $\delta = \varepsilon/K$ always wins the ε–δ game. A model with a small $K$ can't be fooled by tiny input changes (adversarial examples). Spectral normalization and gradient penalties limit $K$.

Limits hide in "temperature"

$\operatorname{softmax}(z/T)$ is smooth for $T>0$. As $T\to 0$ it tends to the discontinuous argmax, the same story as the sigmoid above. Tricks like Gumbel-softmax train with $T>0$ and lower it gradually.

Computers can't take limits

Section 4 shows that very small $h$ makes things worse because of rounding. That is why frameworks use automatic differentiation, which applies the exact derivative rules, and use finite differences only to check gradients.

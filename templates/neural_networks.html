How Neural Networks Learn

A matrix can only re-describe space: it stretches, rotates and shears it, but lines stay straight. A neural network adds a nonlinearity after each matrix, and that lets it bend and fold space until the classes can be split by a straight line. It learns how to bend by gradient descent, and the right change of basis makes that descent much faster.

Why the activation is essential. Without it, two layers give $W_2(W_1x+b_1)+b_2 = (W_2W_1)x + (W_2b_1+b_2)$, which is just one linear layer. However many you stack, lines stay straight, so the only decision boundaries you can get are straight lines.
Questions to ask the student
  1. With None: are the grid lines still straight and evenly spaced after the layer?
  2. With ReLU: where do all the points with both coordinates negative end up? Can you undo that?
  3. tanh squeezes the whole plane into a square. Does it ever send two points to the same place? (No: tanh bends space without folding it.)
  4. Why might Leaky ReLU be preferred over ReLU? (It folds less, so it loses less information and gradients don't die.)

  2. Try Circle inside ring with 2 neurons per layer. Why can't a rubber-sheet bending pull the inner disk out of the ring? (Topology: you'd have to tear or glue the sheet.) So how does the network get there anyway? (It saturates tanh and squashes part of the ring flat: look at the hidden view.)

  4. Reset a few times. Do different weights give the same solution? Why might they not?

3 Gradient descent & preconditioning

Training moves the weights $w$ downhill on a loss surface: $w \leftarrow w - \eta\,\nabla L(w)$. Near a minimum, every loss looks like a bowl $L(w) = \tfrac12 w^{\top}Hw$, whose contours are ellipses. When the bowl is long and thin (a large condition number $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$), the gradient doesn't point at the minimum, so plain gradient descent zig-zags. A change of basis that makes the bowl round fixes this.

Questions to ask the student
  1. Why can't we fix the zig-zag by just lowering η? (The steep direction limits η to below $2/\lambda_{\max}$, and then the flat direction crawls at a rate set by $1/\kappa$.)
  2. Features on different scales: "Rescale each feature" (standardization) works perfectly. Why does it stop working once you add tilt? (When features are correlated, the long axis of the ellipse is diagonal. Rescaling each axis separately can't make it round.)
  3. Adam rescales each coordinate too. Compare it with and without tilt.
  4. Push η·λmax past 2. What happens, and why exactly 2?

4 Takeaways

Matrices re-describe, activations reshape

The linear part of a layer is a change of basis. The nonlinearity is the only thing that changes the shape of the data, so depth without nonlinearity is useless.

Last layer is linear

A classifier network is "learn a representation in which the classes are linearly separable, then apply logistic regression". The hidden layers are a learned feature extractor.

Width matters: topology

Continuous, invertible bending can't untangle some data in low dimensions. A narrow network has to crush information to get around this. Extra neurons add dimensions to move through instead. The kernel trick and wide layers both rely on this.

Conditioning sets the speed

The convergence speed of gradient descent depends on how round the loss bowl is. Standardizing inputs, whitening, batch/layer normalization, Adam and natural gradient all try to change coordinates so the bowl looks rounder.
